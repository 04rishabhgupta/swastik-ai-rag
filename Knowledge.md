5A. NEURO-DIAGNOSTIC TESTS — PATIENT PRE-TEST INSTRUCTIONS AND WHAT TO EXPECT
The site publishes patient instruction pages for each test. These are general instructions; the patient should follow any specific instructions given by the clinic or their doctor.
EEG / VEP / BAER / RNST (evoked-potential instruction page)
Short definitions given by the site
EEG (brain wave test): a graphical record of the brain's electrical activity; helps diagnose and differentiate disorders such as epilepsy, brain infections and sleep disorders.
VEP: measures brain wave activity in response to visual stimulation; helps evaluate diseases related to the eye and brain.
BAER: measures brain wave activity in response to sound; helps evaluate diseases related to the brain and hearing.
Before the test
Tell your doctor about the medicines you are taking.
Wash your hair the night before the test.
Eat your normal diet before the test.
During the test
EEG: a technologist attaches about 16–21 electrodes to the scalp with a conductive, washable paste, placed in a standard pattern. The patient lies or sits comfortably, relaxes and stays still. The patient may be asked to breathe slowly or quickly, and flashing lights or a patterned board may be used.
VEP: electrodes are placed on the scalp and brain activity is recorded in response to visual stimuli. (The site's VEP text also mentions earphones; this appears to be copied from the BAER text.)
BAER: electrodes are placed on the scalp and each earlobe; earphones give brief sounds and the electrodes record brain activity.
The site states the patient does not need to be awake for VEP or BAER.
How long it takes (instruction page)
EEG: usually no more than one hour (long-term monitoring is sometimes used for seizure disorders).
VEP: not more than half an hour.
BAER: not more than half an hour.
After the test
Normal daily activities can be resumed.
Report
The site states the doctor analyses the test and the report is given in a day or two.
EEG is listed for diagnosis of epilepsy and evaluation of symptoms such as:
Episodes of loss of consciousness (sensorium) or unresponsiveness
Abnormal behaviour or change in behaviour
Memory loss / forgetfulness
Confusional state
RNS test is listed for diagnosis of myasthenia gravis and evaluation of weakness and fatigability, drooping eyelids, double vision, difficulty swallowing and slurred speech.
NCS / EMG instruction page
Definitions
NCS (nerve conduction study): measures the speed and strength of electrical activity in a nerve.
EMG (electromyography): records the electrical activity of a muscle at rest and during contraction.
Before the test
Tell your doctor about the medicines you are taking.
Do not apply oil, cream or lotion to the body or limbs.
Do not shave the legs before the test.
Tell the doctor if you are sensitive to electric current or afraid of needles.
Eat your normal diet before the test.
During the test
NCS: the skin is cleaned, electrodes are taped along the nerves, and small electrical stimuli are given at several places. The site says you will feel mild discomfort from the shocks, but it should not be painful.
EMG: a small needle electrode is inserted into a muscle; you are asked to rest and then contract the muscle. It is repeated on different muscles/limbs. The site says insertion may hurt a little and feels like an injection into the muscle.
How long it takes (instruction page)
Upper limbs: about 1 hour (60 minutes)
Lower limbs: about 1.5 hours (90 minutes)
Both upper and lower limbs: about 2.5 hours (150 minutes)
(The Book Online page lists shorter 30-minute slots per study; see section 10.)
After the test
Normal daily activities can be resumed.
After EMG there may be muscle aches/discomfort for several days; the site says warm compresses and pain medicine may help.
Contact your doctor after EMG if there are signs of infection (fever, chills) or redness, swelling, increasing pain, excessive bleeding or discharge at the needle sites.
Report
The site states the report is given in a day or two.
NCS/EMG is listed for evaluation of: numbness, tingling/burning/pins-and-needles, weakness and fatigability, wasting or thinning of muscles, and back pain with radiating pain.

7C. FAQ PAGE — PUBLISHED HEALTH Q&A
The website's FAQ page answers these questions (general education):
Preventing heat-related illness: drink non-alcoholic fluids often, without waiting for thirst (patients whose fluids are restricted or who take water pills should ask their doctor how much to drink); avoid very sugary or very cold drinks; stay in air-conditioned places where possible; fans alone do not prevent heat illness at high temperatures, and a cool shower or bath helps more; wear light, loose, light-coloured clothing; never leave anyone in a parked closed vehicle. Check regularly on infants and young children, people aged 65+, people with mental illness, and people with heart disease or high blood pressure.
Preventing stroke (citing the National Stroke Association): know and control blood pressure; find out about atrial fibrillation; quit smoking; drink alcohol only in moderation; manage diabetes with your doctor; exercise regularly; check cholesterol; eat a lower-salt, lower-fat diet; ask your doctor about circulation problems.
Heart attack warning signs: chest discomfort (pressure, squeezing, fullness or pain lasting more than a few minutes, or that goes away and comes back; it can feel like indigestion); pain or discomfort in one or both arms, back, neck, jaw or upper stomach; shortness of breath; cold sweat, nausea, light-headedness or sudden dizziness; unusual tiredness. Women more often have shortness of breath, nausea/vomiting, and back or jaw pain. People with diabetes may have mild or no symptoms ("silent" heart attack).
Stroke ("brain attack") warning signs: sudden numbness or weakness of the face, arm or leg (especially one side); sudden confusion or trouble speaking/understanding; sudden trouble seeing in one or both eyes; sudden trouble walking, dizziness, loss of balance or coordination; sudden severe headache with no known cause.
RAG rule: if a user describes possible heart-attack or stroke warning signs happening now, tell them to call emergency services (112 in India) or go to the nearest hospital emergency department immediately. Do not suggest booking an OPD visit, ECG or online consultation instead.

7D. SWINE FLU (H1N1) INFORMATION PAGE
A separate information page answers common swine-flu questions:
Swine flu is a viral infection caused by influenza viruses.
Symptoms develop 1–4 days after infection and resemble other flu: fever (100°F or higher), cough, sore throat, runny/stuffy nose, chills, tiredness, headache and body ache.
Symptoms usually last 3–7 days; more serious infection can last up to 10 days.
A patient can be contagious from one day before symptoms to 5–7 days after, sometimes longer.
Higher risk of serious illness: adults over 65, children under 5, people on long-term aspirin, people with weak immunity, pregnant women, and people with chronic illness (diabetes, heart disease, neuromuscular disease, asthma).
Treatment: antiviral medicines with symptomatic medicines (prescribed by a doctor).
Prevention: vaccination is described as the best way to prevent or reduce the chance of influenza infection.

9A. WHEN TO SEEK URGENT CARE — GUIDANCE PUBLISHED IN THE CLINIC'S ARTICLES
These points come from the clinic's own health articles (mainly by Dr Priya Gupta, DM Neurology, and Dr Rahul Gupta, MD Medicine). Use them only to tell users when to seek urgent or emergency care. Do not use them to diagnose.
Stroke / mini-stroke (TIA) — BE-FAST
B – Balance: sudden loss of balance or coordination
E – Eyes: sudden loss of vision or double vision
F – Face: facial drooping
A – Arm: sudden weakness or numbness, especially on one side
S – Speech: slurred speech, difficulty finding words or understanding
T – Time: seek emergency care immediately; do not wait to see if symptoms improve
The TIA article says a TIA must be treated as a medical emergency even if all symptoms disappear, because it can be a warning that a stroke may follow soon.
Headache red flags (SNOOP4) — seek urgent evaluation if headache comes with:
Systemic signs: fever, weight loss, persistent vomiting, night sweats, or a history of cancer, HIV, immunosuppression, transplant or long-term steroids
Neurological signs: weakness on one side, difficulty speaking, double or lost vision, confusion, drowsiness, difficulty walking, seizures, numbness or sudden personality change
Onset that is sudden ("thunderclap": peak within seconds to a minute, "worst headache of my life"). The article calls this a medical emergency.
Older age at onset: a new headache after age 50
Pattern change: headaches becoming more frequent or progressively worse, a migraine pattern that changes, or medicines that no longer work
Positional: a headache that changes with lying down or standing
Seizure first aid (from "First-Time Seizure: What to Do and What to Expect")
Stay calm, move hard or sharp objects away, cushion the head, loosen tight clothing around the neck, turn the person on their side, time the seizure, and stay with them until fully awake.
Do not put anything in the mouth, force the mouth open, give food, water or medicine until fully awake, or hold the person down. People cannot swallow their tongue.
Call an ambulance / seek emergency care if: the seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes; another seizure starts before the person wakes; there is difficulty breathing; the seizure happened in water; there is serious injury; the person is pregnant or diabetic and not recovering normally; it is a first-ever seizure; recovery is unusually slow or confusion persists; or there is new weakness or difficulty speaking afterwards.
One seizure does not necessarily mean epilepsy, but a first seizure always needs medical evaluation.
Fever (from "When to worry about a fever?")
Fever is a temperature above 100.4°F (38°C). How sick the person feels matters more than the exact number.
Consult a doctor if the person has fever and: frequent infections, chemotherapy, pregnancy, recent travel to Africa, Latin America or the Middle East, immune-suppressing medicines such as steroids, recent surgery, a recent insect bite, or a serious condition (diabetes, heart disease, cancer, lupus, sickle cell anaemia).
Also consult if the temperature stays above 100.4°F for more than 1 hour or goes above 101°F even once, or if fever lasts several days or keeps returning.
Seek care urgently for fever with rash, breathing difficulty or chest heaviness, severe headache or neck pain, severe or repeated vomiting or diarrhoea, severe pain, seizure, confusion or unconsciousness, or a change in behaviour.
Heat-related illness (from "Symptoms of Heat Related Illness")
Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, weakness, cold, pale and clammy skin, fast weak pulse, nausea or vomiting, fainting.
Heat stroke: body temperature above 103°F, hot red dry or moist skin, rapid strong pulse, possible unconsciousness. Heat stroke is an emergency.
